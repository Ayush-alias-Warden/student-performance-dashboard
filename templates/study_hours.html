Critical Evaluation: Avoiding the Causation Fallacy (BL5)

A central requirement of rigorous data science is acknowledging that correlation does not equal causation. While the empirical data demonstrates a consistent, positive correlation between study hours and exam performance, claiming that "assigning more study hours directly causes higher grades" would be invalid for the following reasons:

  • Low Coefficient of Determination (R2 ≈ 0.026 to 0.036): Study time accounts for less than 4% of total score variability. Over 96% of variance is driven by unmodeled factors.
  • Omitted Confounding Variables: Students who study more frequently possess higher baseline motivation, stronger parental encouragement (Medu/Fedu), and fewer school absences.
  • Quality vs. Quantity of Study: Self-reported study hours measure time seated, not cognitive efficiency. A student practicing active retrieval for 2 hours may outperform a student passively rereading notes for 10 hours.
  • Reverse / Bidirectional Causality: Students who grasp the material more easily may find studying rewarding and therefore report higher study hours, creating reverse causation.

Conclusion: Study hours serve as a valuable diagnostic marker of student commitment, but educational interventions must combine study support with foundational skills and attendance tracking.
